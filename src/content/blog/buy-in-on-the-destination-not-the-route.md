On 19 November, Lawrence Chua and I were the guests at the closing fireside dialogue of SMU Executive Development's Team Lead Programme. Dr Faisal Aman, the programme director, moderated and set the theme: agility and effective management in the age of disruptions. The class was team leads from eight organisations and six nationalities, on the last afternoon of a three-day programme. They had spent the week on simulations, negotiation and design thinking, and they came with sharp questions.

We covered what agility means when a client wants AI without breaking what already works, how to deal with the anxiety that comes with all this change, and which disruptions businesses are least prepared for. Then the class took over.

## What the class asked

- How do you use AI without losing the human touch?
- People are already harvesting encrypted data today, waiting for quantum computers to crack it later. How should we think about security?
- Which leadership qualities stay important, even irreplaceable, in the age of AI?
- What has changed in your own day-to-day work?
- How do we build redundancy and resilience?
- Now that experimenting is cheaper, how much should we let teams experiment?
- When the direction changes, does the whole team need to buy in, or is it fine if a few people don't?

I may come back to some of these. This post is about the last one, because I have not stopped thinking about it.

## The short answer

No, not on everything. You need buy-in on the destination, not on the route.

The destination is what we are trying to achieve: the outcome, the customer, the reason the change is happening at all. Everyone on the team needs to agree on that, or you will feel the friction in every meeting.

The route is how we get there: the design, the sequence, which team does what. People can disagree on the route, and they should. That disagreement is healthy. It is how teams find better routes. A team where nobody ever argues about the how is usually a team where people have stopped telling you what they think.

## The time I was the one who did not buy in

I want to be honest here, because I learned this the hard way, and not as the leader trying to win people over.

A while back, a VP I reported to kept warning us that part of our design would create too much traffic. Our software checked in with the cloud very often. Engineers call these check-ins heartbeats. He was worried that at scale, all those heartbeats would add up to a problem.

My team pushed back. Another team's service was the one handling those check-ins. If the load was a problem, we felt, they should scale up. It was their service, so it was their fix. We did not buy in.

We were wrong. Customers started noticing the extra traffic leaving their own networks, and they were not happy about it. To them, it did not matter whose service was handling the heartbeats. We were the face they saw. In the end we worked with the other team to cut the heartbeats down, which is what our VP had been asking for in the first place.

Looking back, we had been arguing about the route, whose service should change, and we lost sight of the destination: a customer who is happy with what we run for them. If I had asked myself one question earlier, "are we disagreeing about the destination or the route?", I think we would have got there much sooner.

## Start by listening

So when someone on your team does not buy in, my first advice is to find out why, properly.

In my experience, the people who push back openly are rarely the problem. They are usually passionate about the work. Often they are also credible: they have earned their standing, which is why they feel confident enough to say no out loud. That also means they may have real influence in the team, sometimes beyond it. Ignoring them is expensive. Listening to them is cheap, and sometimes, as my VP showed me, they are simply right.

When you listen, sort what you hear into the two buckets. Is this person questioning where we are going, or how we are getting there? Those need very different conversations.

## Two ways forward

If the disagreement is real, a team lead usually has two good options.

- **Win them over.** Explain the why again, with the context they may not have. Show them what you are protecting and what you are willing to drop.
- **Reshape the plan.** Keep the destination, but change the route so it deals with what worries them. You may not close the gap in one step. That is fine. Close it step by step.

## Watch for the quiet yes

Even after someone says yes, keep checking whether they are genuinely on board or just going with the flow.

Words are easy. Actions tell you more. A decision that keeps taking longer than it should. An implementation that slows down for no clear reason. A meeting where everyone nods and nothing moves afterwards. Quiet resistance is harder to deal with than open disagreement, because it never puts its hand up.

## When it still does not resolve

Sometimes you listen, you reshape, and you still cannot get there. Then be honest with your own boss. The people above you have invested in this direction, and they are counting on an outcome from your team. They cannot help with a problem they do not know about. Leading through change means managing up as well as down.

And sometimes, after all of that, the right answer is that a person would serve better on a different team or a different project. In a big organisation, that can be the best outcome for everyone. I want to be careful how I say this, because it is the last step, not the first. Their quality does not change because they disagreed with one direction. It just might not be the right fit for this one.

## What I am taking back

The next time someone pushes back on me, or I find myself pushing back, I want to ask one question before anything else: are we disagreeing about the destination, or the route?

If it is the route, good. Let us argue it out. If it is the destination, that is the conversation to have first.
